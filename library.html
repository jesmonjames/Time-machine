Classified catalogue of field reading materials — texts that shaped the operative's thinking across engineering, creativity & philosophy.

ALL DESIGN PHILOSOPHY ENGINEERING ART PSYCHOLOGY
The Design of Everyday Things
— Don Norman · DESIGN / UX

A foundational text on human-centred design. Explores why some products satisfy users while others frustrate them, and how design can communicate its own use.

The book introduced the concept of affordances — the relationship between a user and an object. It fundamentally changed how I approach interface architecture. Every element on a screen should communicate its own interaction model without requiring instruction. This idea drives every UI decision I make.
Meditations
— Marcus Aurelius · PHILOSOPHY / STOICISM

Private journal of a Roman emperor — a manual on controlling perception, directing action with purpose, and accepting what cannot be changed.

Written for no audience but himself, Aurelius' meditations become a timeless field manual for navigating chaos. The practice of separating what is within our control from what is not has direct applications to both engineering under pressure and creative work during failure. Essential reading.
The Artist's Way
— Julia Cameron · ART / CREATIVITY

A 12-week program for recovering and discovering creative self. Introduces the concept of morning pages and the artist's date as tools for creative recovery.

This book dismantled the internal censors that block creative flow. The morning pages practice — three uncensored, handwritten pages each morning — became a mechanism to separate noise from genuine creative impulse. It connected the technical and artistic sides of my practice in unexpected ways.
Clean Code
— Robert C. Martin · ENGINEERING / SOFTWARE

A handbook for writing readable, maintainable code. Argues that writing clean code is a professional responsibility, not just a preference.

The book's central argument — that code is read far more often than it is written — reshaped how I think about variable naming, function scope, and component structure. The metaphor of code as a newspaper article, where the important summary is at the top, directly influenced how I architect component trees.
Thinking, Fast and Slow
— Daniel Kahneman · PSYCHOLOGY / BEHAVIOUR

Explores the two systems that drive the way we think — System 1 (fast, intuitive) and System 2 (slow, deliberate) — and the biases they produce.

Understanding cognitive biases changed how I design user interfaces. Users operate primarily in System 1 — they react before they think. Designing for this means reducing friction, using familiar patterns, and ensuring that dangerous actions require deliberate, System 2 engagement. This book is a UX bible in disguise.
Ways of Seeing
— John Berger · ART / VISUAL CULTURE

A radical analysis of how we look at images and how our cultural conditioning shapes visual perception — based on the landmark BBC television series.

Berger argues that we never simply look at art — we are already carrying assumptions about gender, class, and social power that shape every perception. This awareness fundamentally altered how I compose visual work, asking: who is this made for, and what does the framing convey without words?
Atomic Habits
— James Clear · PHILOSOPHY / BEHAVIOUR

A proven framework for building good habits and breaking bad ones. Argues that systems, not goals, are the foundation of lasting improvement.

The core insight — that identity is shaped by the small actions we repeat, not by our aspirations — changed how I approach learning. Instead of aiming to "become a better developer," I focused on daily habits: one component built, one concept documented. The compound effect is real and measurable over time.
Don't Make Me Think
— Steve Krug · DESIGN / UX / WEB

The definitive guide to web usability. Argues that effective web design removes cognitive load so users can navigate with minimal effort.

Krug's first law — "Don't make me think" — sounds simple but is ruthlessly hard to execute. Every element that forces users to pause and question is a failure of design. This book recalibrated my instinct for when to add and when to remove, which in practice means removing far more than feels comfortable.
